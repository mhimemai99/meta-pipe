# Introduction

Sustained cognitive work is ubiquitously accompanied by a subjective sense of fatigue and, in many individuals, by physical discomfort — commonly described as head pressure, heaviness behind the eyes, or a diffuse headache-like sensation. This experience is so common that it is rarely questioned: knowledge workers, students, and professionals routinely attribute headaches to "thinking too hard." Yet the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain poorly understood, in large part because the relevant scientific literatures are fragmented across disciplines that rarely communicate.

The question that motivates this scoping review is not a clinical one. We do not ask why patients with tension-type headache experience headache during stress (a well-studied clinical question). Rather, we ask: **Why does sustained cognitive effort produce a discomfort signal in healthy individuals, and why does that signal sometimes manifest as a headache-like somatic experience?**

This question sits at the intersection of at least five research domains, each of which has developed substantially in recent years but in relative isolation from the others.

## Five Literature Layers

**Layer 1: Why cognitive effort is aversive.** Beginning with Kurzban et al.'s (2013) opportunity cost model and formalized by Shenhav et al.'s (2013) Expected Value of Control (EVC) framework, a substantial theoretical and empirical literature now addresses why cognitive effort feels costly. Effort aversion is understood as a rational signal: engaging in one task forecloses other activities, and the subjective sense of effort indexes this opportunity cost. Inzlicht et al. (2018) further complicated this picture by demonstrating the "effort paradox" — effort is both prospectively avoided and retrospectively valued.

**Layer 2: The neural metabolic cost of sustained cognition.** Wiehler et al. (2022) provided landmark evidence that glutamate accumulates in the lateral prefrontal cortex during a day of sustained cognitive control, driving a shift toward low-effort economic choices. Earlier theoretical work by Johansson and Ronnback (2004) and Christie and Schrater (2015) proposed energetic accounts of cognitive cost involving glutamate transport and astrocytic glycogen, respectively. This layer suggests that cognitive effort has genuine metabolic consequences in the brain.

**Layer 3: Effort as an aversive signal.** The dACC has been identified as the brain's "effort meter," computing the expected value of control and signaling when the cost of further cognitive engagement exceeds its expected benefit (Shenhav et al., 2013, 2016). Yee et al. (2022) extended this framework to show how aversive motivation modulates cognitive control via dACC–dopamine–serotonin interactions. Hogan et al. (2024) identified dlPFC–insula coupling as the mechanism by which cognitive exertion signals influence effort-based choices under fatigue.

**Layer 4: Predictive processing and allostatic framing.** The free energy principle and active inference framework (Seth & Friston, 2016; Seth, 2013) provide a compelling theoretical bridge: bodily states are actively predicted, and interoceptive prediction errors generate aversive feelings. Stephan et al. (2016) explicitly proposed that fatigue is a metacognitive signal of dyshomeostasis — the brain's assessment that its current metabolic trajectory is unsustainable. Barrett et al. (2016) extended this to a general theory of allostatic interoception.

**Layer 5: Somatic referral of cognitive fatigue.** The headache medicine literature has documented that mental stress lowers pain thresholds in tension-type headache patients via central sensitization (Cathcart et al., 2010). However, this literature is almost exclusively focused on clinical headache populations, leaving the healthy-adult phenomenon unaddressed.

## Rationale for a Scoping Review

A meta-analysis of this literature is not feasible. Outcomes are heterogeneously operationalized (VAS fatigue, effort discounting, pericranial tenderness, glutamate concentration), manipulations vary widely (15-minute Stroop to 6-hour simulated workday), and the target population — healthy adults experiencing discomfort during cognitive work — is nearly absent from existing empirical studies. A scoping review is the appropriate methodology to map this heterogeneous, cross-disciplinary terrain, make existing gaps visible, and position future experimental work.

## Objective

The objective of this scoping review is to systematically map the existing literature on somatic discomfort during sustained cognitive effort in healthy adults, identify which theoretical frameworks have been applied, and delineate critical research gaps that must be addressed before the relationship between effortful cognition and somatic discomfort can be empirically tested.
