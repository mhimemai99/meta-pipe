# Discussion

This scoping review mapped 39 studies across five literature layers addressing the relationship between sustained cognitive effort and somatic discomfort in healthy adults. The central finding is paradoxical: while substantial theoretical and empirical work addresses *why cognitive effort is aversive* (L1, L3) and *what metabolic changes accompany sustained cognition* (L2), the somatic dimension — the actual discomfort that people experience — is almost entirely unstudied. Only one included study measured head-specific discomfort during a cognitive task, and no study has jointly measured effort economics and somatic localization.

## Integration Across Layers

The five-layer structure of this review reflects the genuine fragmentation of the field. Effort economists (L1) have developed sophisticated models of why people avoid cognitive demand, but have not asked participants where they feel discomfort. Neurometabolic researchers (L2) have identified glutamate accumulation as a consequence of sustained cognitive work, but have not connected this to subjective experience. Interoceptive theorists (L4) have proposed that discomfort reflects interoceptive prediction error, but have not tested this in the cognitive fatigue context. Headache researchers (L5) have characterized pericranial mechanisms, but almost exclusively in clinical populations.

The revised conceptual model proposed in this review — from glutamate accumulation through interoceptive prediction error to conscious discomfort — represents the first explicit integration of these five literatures. Each individual link has theoretical or partial empirical support, but no single study has tested the chain from metabolic change to somatic experience.

## Why the Gap Persists

Several factors explain why the somatic dimension of cognitive effort has been neglected:

First, cognitive effort research has been dominated by behavioral economics and computational neuroscience perspectives (L1, L3), which conceptualize effort as a decision variable — something to be avoided or valued — rather than as a bodily sensation. The subjective phenomenology of effort is treated as an input to a decision function, not as an experience to be characterized.

Second, the headache/pain literature (L5) is a clinical discipline oriented toward understanding pathological headache in patients, not everyday discomfort in healthy populations. The 4% headache rate observed in Cathcart et al.'s (2010) healthy controls was noted but not pursued as a research question.

Third, the predictive processing literature (L4) has developed primarily at the theoretical and computational level, with limited empirical testing even in its core domains. Applying this framework to the specific case of cognitive fatigue discomfort would require novel experimental paradigms that cross traditional disciplinary boundaries.

## The Key Insight

The discomfort of hard thinking is not pain mistakenly generated by the brain. Rather, it is best understood as a correctly generated allostatic signal: the brain detects that its current metabolic trajectory is unsustainable (L2), computes that the cost of further control exceeds the benefit (L3), and surfaces this assessment as a conscious interoceptive signal (L4) that motivates behavioral change (L1). The headache-like quality of this experience in some individuals may reflect somatic labeling of the interoceptive prediction error, shaped by prior experience, attentional interpretation, and individual differences in pericranial sensitivity (L5).

This framing reorients the question from "What is wrong with my brain?" to "What signal is my brain correctly generating, and why?"

## Implications for Future Research

### Priority Experiment

The most valuable single study that could emerge from this review would jointly measure effort-based decision shifts and somatic discomfort localization during sustained cognitive work. A within-subjects design with healthy adults performing high-demand (3-back) versus low-demand cognitive tasks for 45+ minutes, with periodic VAS assessments of discomfort location (head, neck, general, none) alongside post-task effort discounting measures, would address the central gap identified in this review.

### Additional Research Priorities

1. **Computational fMRI study**: Measuring interoceptive prediction error during cognitive fatigue accumulation would bridge L2 and L4, testing whether the allostatic model can account for the subjective experience of cognitive fatigue.

2. **Individual differences study**: Examining need for cognition, interoceptive accuracy, and migraine vulnerability as moderators would clarify why some individuals experience head-specific discomfort while others experience only diffuse fatigue.

3. **Ecological validity**: Extending paradigms to real-world sustained cognitive work (knowledge work, programming, screen-based sustained attention) would increase the applicability of findings.

## Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the search was limited to PubMed and Semantic Scholar, potentially missing relevant studies indexed in discipline-specific databases (PsycINFO, Embase). However, the cross-disciplinary nature of the search strategy and citation tracing mitigate this concern. Second, the high proportion of theoretical papers (59%) reflects the state of the field rather than a search limitation — empirical work at the intersection of these five domains is genuinely sparse. Third, we did not conduct formal quality appraisal, consistent with JBI guidance for scoping reviews but limiting our ability to weight evidence strength. Fourth, the charting process relied on abstracts and available metadata rather than full-text extraction for all studies, which may have resulted in incomplete capture of some study details. Fifth, the broad inclusion criteria inherent to scoping reviews means that included studies vary substantially in their direct relevance to the core research question.

## Conclusions

This scoping review reveals that the experience of somatic discomfort during sustained cognitive effort — a nearly universal human experience — sits in a research blind spot at the intersection of five established but disconnected literatures. The theoretical tools to understand this phenomenon exist (opportunity cost models, neuro-metabolic accounts, interoceptive inference, allostatic regulation), but they have not been brought together empirically. The field is not ready for meta-analysis; it is ready for the targeted empirical work that this review makes possible.
