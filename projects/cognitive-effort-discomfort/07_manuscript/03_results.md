# Results

## Search Results

The search identified 43 records across five query sets. After deduplication (0 duplicates removed), 43 unique records were screened at the title/abstract level. Four records were excluded (all coded P1: exclusively clinical headache/pain populations), yielding 39 studies for inclusion and charting. Inter-rater agreement was substantial (Cohen's kappa = 0.80, 93.0% overall agreement).

## Characteristics of Included Studies

Of the 39 included studies, 16 (41.0%) were empirical studies and 23 (59.0%) were theoretical papers, reviews, commentaries, or computational modeling studies. The median publication year was 2018 (range: 2004–2025). Studies originated primarily from the USA (n=12), UK (n=8), France (n=4), and other European countries (n=10).

### Literature Layer Distribution

Studies were distributed across the five layers as follows (note: seven studies were classified into multiple layers):
- **L1 — Effort cost mechanisms:** 15 studies (38.5%)
- **L2 — Neural metabolic cost:** 8 studies (20.5%)
- **L3 — Effort as aversive signal:** 8 studies (20.5%)
- **L4 — Predictive processing/allostasis:** 9 studies (23.1%)
- **L5 — Somatic referral:** 1 study (2.6%)

The pronounced asymmetry, with L5 represented by a single study, reflects the central finding of this review: the somatic dimension of cognitive effort discomfort is nearly unstudied.

### Study Designs

Among the 16 empirical studies, 10 used within-subjects designs, 4 used between-subjects or mixed designs, 1 was a randomized crossover trial, and 1 was a case-control study. Task types included N-back (n=3), sustained attention tasks (n=2), Stroop variants (n=2), demand selection tasks (n=2), effort discounting paradigms (n=3), and other cognitive tasks. Task durations ranged from 30 to 375 minutes, with a median of approximately 60 minutes.

### Outcome Measures

**Discomfort/fatigue:** Twelve studies (30.8%) measured some form of subjective discomfort or fatigue, most commonly using Visual Analogue Scales (VAS) for fatigue or discomfort. Only one study (Cathcart et al., 2010; 2.6%) assessed head-specific somatic localization.

**Effort cost:** Sixteen studies (41.0%) measured effort cost, most commonly through effort discounting tasks (n=5), subjective effort ratings (n=6), or demand avoidance paradigms (n=3).

**Neural/metabolic markers:** Fifteen studies (38.5%) included neuroimaging or neurophysiological measures: fMRI (n=5), magnetic resonance spectroscopy (n=4), EEG (n=3), and pupillometry (n=3).

**Somatic localization:** Only one study (2.6%) directly assessed where participants felt discomfort (head/pericranial). Ten studies (25.6%) measured "general fatigue" without localization. Seven studies (17.9%) measured neural or behavioral outcomes without assessing somatic symptoms. Twenty-one studies (53.8%) were theoretical or review papers.

## Narrative Synthesis by Layer

### Layer 1: Why Cognitive Effort Is Aversive

The opportunity cost model (Kurzban et al., 2013) proposed that effort aversion reflects the foreclosed value of alternative activities rather than resource depletion. The Expected Value of Control framework (Shenhav et al., 2013, 2017) formalized this computationally, with the dACC integrating payoff, control demands, and effort costs. Kool et al. (2010) provided the first behavioral economic demonstration that people systematically avoid cognitive demand when given a choice. The effort paradox (Inzlicht et al., 2018) revealed that effort is paradoxically both prospectively avoided and retrospectively valued.

Empirically, Massar et al. (2018) demonstrated that 90 minutes of sustained N-back performance increased effort discounting, and Muller et al. (2023) showed computationally that fatigue fluctuates trial-by-trial as a function of exerted effort, with cognitive errors specifically inducing momentary fatigue increases.

### Layer 2: Neural Metabolic Cost

Wiehler et al. (2022) provided the landmark finding: approximately 6 hours of sustained cognitive control work produced glutamate accumulation in the lateral prefrontal cortex, measured by MRS, accompanied by a behavioral shift toward low-effort economic choices. This neuro-metabolic account proposed that cognitive fatigue reflects the necessity of recycling potentially toxic byproducts of neural activity. Blain and Pessiglione (2023) offered a critical evaluation, noting that the MRS-detected changes may reflect intercompartmental redistribution rather than absolute extracellular accumulation. Gavelin et al. (2020) provided clinical convergence, finding elevated ACC/mPFC glutamate in patients with occupational exhaustion syndrome, linearly correlated with perceived stress.

### Layer 3: Effort as Aversive Signal

The dACC emerged as the central neural substrate of effort monitoring. Shenhav et al. (2013, 2016) demonstrated that dACC integrates payoff, control, and effort cost to compute the expected value of control. Otto et al. (2018) identified a left-lateralized network (anterior insula, ACC, striatum) that tracks both task demand and fatigue state during a simulated workday. Hogan et al. (2024) found that cognitive fatigue specifically reduces the willingness to exert cognitive effort for reward, mediated by dlPFC–insula functional coupling.

### Layer 4: Predictive Processing and Allostasis

Seth and Friston (2016) elaborated the active interoceptive inference framework: bodily states are actively predicted, and interoceptive prediction errors generate aversive feelings. Stephan et al. (2016) explicitly proposed that fatigue is a metacognitive signal of dyshomeostasis, driven by divergence between expected and actual interoceptive states. Barrett et al. (2016) extended this to a theory of allostatic interoception. Wolff et al. (2019) provided empirical evidence linking self-control to interoceptive inference, showing that self-control failures occur when predicted interoceptive models are inaccurate.

### Layer 5: Somatic Referral

Only one included study addressed the somatic dimension. Cathcart et al. (2010) exposed both tension-type headache patients and healthy controls to a 60-minute mental arithmetic stress task. Headache developed in 91% of patients but only 4% of controls, with increased pericranial tenderness and decreased pain thresholds in both groups. The stress-hyperalgesia mechanism was supported: cognitive stress amplifies existing nociceptive sensitivity.

## Gap Analysis

Six critical research gaps were identified:

1. **Joint measurement gap:** No study has simultaneously measured effort-based decision shifts and somatic discomfort localization (complete gap).
2. **Somatic localization gap:** Only 1/39 studies (2.6%) assessed head-specific discomfort during a cognitive task.
3. **Metabolic–interoceptive bridge:** No study has experimentally connected metabolic changes (e.g., glutamate) to interoceptive prediction error (complete gap).
4. **Healthy adult headache data:** Only one study included healthy adults in a headache-relevant cognitive stress paradigm.
5. **Individual differences:** No study examined moderators (need for cognition, interoceptive accuracy, migraine vulnerability) of the effort–discomfort relationship.
6. **Ecological validity:** Only one study (Wiehler et al., 2022) approximated real-world workday duration; no studies examined remote work or screen-based sustained cognition.

## Proposed Conceptual Model

Integrating across all five layers, a revised conceptual model emerges:

Sustained cognitive control demand leads to glutamate accumulation in the lateral prefrontal cortex and glycogen depletion in astrocytes (L2). As lPFC activation becomes metabolically costly, the EVC signal in dACC reflects that the cost of further control exceeds the expected benefit (L3). This generates an interoceptive prediction error: the internal metabolic state deviates from the predicted optimum (L4). The discomfort is surfaced to conscious awareness via the anterior insula and dACC (L4). In individuals without peripheral sensitization, this manifests as vague fatigue and motivational withdrawal (L1). In sensitized or migraine-prone individuals, pericranial referral may produce a headache-like experience (L5).

This model is theoretically coherent but empirically untested at every bridging point between layers.
