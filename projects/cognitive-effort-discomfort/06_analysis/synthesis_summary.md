# Narrative Synthesis Summary

## Overview
39 studies were included in this scoping review, spanning 5 literature layers. This synthesis maps the current evidence landscape and identifies how the layers relate to each other.

---

## Layer 1: Why Effort Is Aversive (15 studies, 38.5%)

The largest body of work addresses the fundamental question of why cognitive effort feels costly. Three theoretical positions dominate:

**Opportunity Cost Model (Kurzban et al., 2013)**: Effort feels aversive because engaging in one task forecloses other activities. The subjective experience of "fatigue" is a motivating signal, not a reflection of resource depletion. This model successfully explains why fatigue varies with task value rather than objective difficulty.

**Expected Value of Control (Shenhav et al., 2013, 2017)**: The dACC computes the expected value of control by integrating payoff, required control, and effort cost. When the cost of further control exceeds expected benefit, the system signals to disengage. This provides a neural mechanism for the opportunity cost model.

**Effort Paradox (Inzlicht et al., 2018)**: Effort is both costly (prospectively discounted) and valued (retrospectively appreciated). This resolves contradictions in the literature where people both avoid and seek effortful activities.

**Empirical support**: Kool et al. (2010) demonstrated that people systematically avoid cognitive demand. Massar et al. (2018) showed that cognitive fatigue increases effort discounting. Muller et al. (2023) provided a computational model showing fatigue fluctuates trial-by-trial as a function of exerted effort.

**Key limitation**: These studies measure effort aversion behaviorally (choice, discounting) but do not assess somatic discomfort or its localization.

---

## Layer 2: Neural Metabolic Cost of Sustained Cognition (8 studies, 20.5%)

**Landmark finding**: Wiehler et al. (2022) used MRS to show that glutamate accumulates in the lateral prefrontal cortex during a day of sustained cognitive control work. This accumulation was associated with a shift toward low-effort, low-delay economic choices — providing the first neurometabolic account of cognitive fatigue.

**Theoretical underpinnings**: Johansson & Ronnback (2004) proposed that extracellular glutamate accumulation inhibits brainstem nuclei (LC, raphe), reducing noradrenaline and serotonin release. Christie & Schrater (2015) modeled astrocytic glycogen as the energetic buffer, arguing that the brain rationally conserves activity to manage energy budgets.

**Challenges**: Blain & Pessiglione (2023) critically evaluated the glutamate hypothesis, noting that MRS captures total glutamate (intra + extracellular) and the observed effects may be driven by decreases in controls rather than increases in fatigued participants. Gavelin et al. (2020) found elevated glutamate in ACC/mPFC of exhaustion syndrome patients, providing clinical convergence.

**Key limitation**: No study has connected metabolic changes to subjective discomfort experience. Wiehler measured economic decision shifts, not somatic symptoms.

---

## Layer 3: Effort as Aversive Signal — dACC and Monitoring (8 studies, 20.5%)

This layer bridges L1 (why effort is costly) with neural mechanisms:

**EVC model (Shenhav et al., 2013, 2016)**: The dACC functions as the brain's "effort meter," integrating payoff, control demands, and effort costs to determine optimal control allocation. When costs exceed benefits, control is withdrawn.

**Aversive motivation (Yee et al., 2022)**: Aversive motivation modulates cognitive control via dACC–dopamine–serotonin interactions. This extends the EVC framework to explain why punishment and threat signals can sometimes *enhance* control allocation.

**Neural substrates of fatigue (Otto et al., 2018; Hogan et al., 2024)**: fMRI studies show that a left-lateralized network (anterior insula, ACC, striatum) tracks both task demand and fatigue state. Hogan et al. identified dlPFC–insula coupling as the mechanism by which cognitive exertion signals influence effort-based choices.

**Key limitation**: The aversive signal literature characterizes effort monitoring but does not address why the signal sometimes manifests as somatic discomfort (head pressure, headache-like sensation) rather than purely motivational withdrawal.

---

## Layer 4: Predictive Processing and Allostatic Framing (9 studies, 23.1%)

This layer provides the most promising theoretical bridge from effort monitoring (L3) to somatic discomfort (L5):

**Active interoceptive inference (Seth & Friston, 2016; Seth, 2013)**: Bodily states are actively predicted by the brain. When predictions fail (interoceptive prediction error), aversive feelings are generated — including discomfort and pain. Sustained cognitive effort could create interoceptive prediction error as the internal state deviates from the predicted optimum.

**Allostatic fatigue model (Stephan et al., 2016; Barrett et al., 2016)**: Fatigue is a metacognitive signal of dyshomeostasis — it reflects the brain's assessment that its current metabolic trajectory is unsustainable. This directly connects to L2 (glutamate accumulation shifts the metabolic trajectory) and L1 (the signal motivates behavioral change).

**Self-control and interoception (Wolff et al., 2019)**: Self-control failures occur when predicted interoceptive models are inaccurate, directly linking cognitive control (L3) to interoceptive inference (L4).

**Key limitation**: This is the most theoretical layer — no study has experimentally measured interoceptive prediction error during cognitive fatigue. The bridge from interoceptive PE to conscious discomfort during cognitive work is entirely inferential.

---

## Layer 5: Somatic Referral of Cognitive Fatigue (1 study, 2.6%)

The thinnest layer. Only one included study (Cathcart et al., 2010) addressed the headache/somatic pain dimension:

**Cathcart et al. (2010)**: Mental arithmetic stress task (60 min) produced headache in 91% of TTH patients but only 4% of healthy controls. Pericranial tenderness increased and pain thresholds decreased during the task. This supports a central sensitization mechanism: stress lowers pain thresholds, and in individuals with pre-existing pericranial sensitivity, the threshold for headache is crossed.

**Key implication**: The near-absence of healthy adult data means that whether cognitive effort produces head-specific discomfort in non-clinical populations is essentially unknown. The 4% headache rate in Cathcart's healthy controls is tantalizing but unstudied further.

---

## Cross-Layer Integration: The Revised Conceptual Model

Synthesizing across all 5 layers, the following causal chain emerges:

```
L2: Sustained cognitive control → glutamate accumulation in lPFC + glycogen depletion
    ↓
L3: lPFC activation becomes metabolically costly → EVC signal in dACC: cost exceeds benefit
    ↓
L4: Interoceptive prediction error: internal state deviates from predicted optimum
    ↓
L1: Discomfort surfaced to consciousness → motivational withdrawal / effort discounting
    ↓
L5: In sensitized individuals: pericranial referral → headache
    In non-sensitized: vague fatigue + motivational withdrawal
```

**Critical insight**: The discomfort of hard thinking is not pain mistakenly generated by the brain — it is a correctly generated allostatic signal telling the organism to stop and recover. The headache quality in non-clinical individuals may be a somatic labeling of interoceptive prediction error, shaped by prior experience and attentional interpretation.

**This model is not yet experimentally validated.** The cross-layer connections (L2→L4, L4→L5) have zero empirical support. The scoping review makes this gap explicitly visible.
